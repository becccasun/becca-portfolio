<Section id="overview" title="You could spend forty minutes on an application you were never eligible to make.">
  <Prose>
    Opening a Loop account means a KYB application: legal entity details,
    every director and beneficial owner over 25%, proof of operations,
    government ID for each person, expected payment volumes, and a stack of
    documents. It is long because it has to be. Loop is a regulated financial
    institution in Canada and compliance sets most of the fields.

    What compliance does not set is the order. The old flow collected all of
    it, then queued the application for human review, and the applicant found
    out days later whether Loop could serve their business at all. Cannabis,
    crypto, a Quebec sole proprietorship, no Canadian resident director: all of
    these are policy limits Loop knows about in advance, and all of them were
    being discovered at the end.
  </Prose>
</Section>

<Section id="data" title="What the data said.">
  <Prose>
    I pulled the baseline before designing anything, from application decision
    data and the support and product analytics Loop already had.

    Thirty-nine percent of onboarding decisions were declines. The median wait
    for a decision was six business days and the ninetieth percentile was
    thirty-four. Around forty-two percent of people who started registration
    never finished it. And of the applicants who did get approved, the share
    who went on to activate their account had fallen from sixty-nine percent to
    forty-one.

    The last number is the one that reframed the project for me. The people in
    that gap had already been told yes. Something between approval and first
    use was losing customers Loop had already paid to acquire and already
    decided to accept.
  </Prose>

  <Prose>
    Then I went through the decline reasons themselves. Restricted industry,
    prohibited entity type, Quebec sole proprietorship, no Canadian director,
    applicant under 18, pre-revenue, spam. Nearly all of the top reasons are
    answerable from five questions, and none of them require a document.
  </Prose>

  <PullQuote>
    How might we tell someone we cannot serve them in seconds, instead of after
    six days and thirty uploaded files?
  </PullQuote>
</Section>

<Section id="eligibility" title="Screening moved in front of the review queue.">
  <Prose>
    The priority move of the redesign is a single screen placed before document
    upload and before anything reaches a human reviewer. Industry, a restricted
    activity checklist, province and entity type, whether at least one director
    is a Canadian resident, and whether the business is generating revenue yet.

    Everything downstream changed shape once that screen existed. Business
    details pre-fill from answers already given. The registry check runs
    automatically against public records to confirm the entity exists and is in
    good standing, which takes seconds and used to be part of what people
    waited days for. Ineligible applicants never enter the queue, so the queue
    itself gets shorter for everyone still in it.

    Screening also turned out to be the hardest part to design, because the
    branching kept growing. Businesses the registry cannot identify need
    conditional jurisdiction, entity type and CRA number steps. I ended up
    mapping the whole eligibility tree in FigJam as its own artifact, separate
    from the screens, because the logic stopped fitting in a linear flow.
  </Prose>
</Section>

<Section id="declines" title="Every no has a reason, a person to talk to, and a way back.">
  <Prose>
    Front-loading the screening only helps if the rejection itself is decent.
    The old exits were generic. The redesign gives every terminal state
    reason-specific copy, an alternative where one exists, and a route to a
    human.

    It also separates three things the old flow treated the same way. A hard
    policy limit is a terminal exit. A borderline case, like a pre-revenue
    business, gets an honest heads-up and a choice: talk to someone first, or
    continue flagged for genuine review. Never a silent surprise decline later.
    An unreadable document is neither: it is a recapture loop with tips, not a
    rejection, and it never restarts the application.

    The same rule applies after submission. When review needs one more
    document, the screen asks for exactly that document inline and preserves
    everything else. And "Under review" is designed as a real screen rather
    than a gap: what is happening, an honest ranged estimate, what you can
    usefully do in the meantime, and how you will hear. A median six day wait
    with a ninetieth percentile of thirty-four cannot be a blank page.
  </Prose>
</Section>

<Section id="cofill" title="One person should not have to hold the entire application.">
  <Prose>
    Watching where applications stalled, a pattern showed up that had nothing
    to do with eligibility. The person filling out the form frequently does not
    have all the answers. They need the CFO's numbers, a director's ID, a
    registration number somebody else has.

    So the application became something more than one person can work on.
    Invited owners and signers verify their own identity through secure email
    links on their own time, in parallel, and the applicant is never blocked
    waiting for them. Beyond that, any field, subsection, or whole page can be
    sent to a teammate: it locks, shows "waiting for Priya to fill", and can be
    revoked and taken back. The system still records who entered and edited
    what, because compliance needs that audit trail regardless of how many
    people touched the form.

    Getting there took a wrong turn first. I initially designed simultaneous
    editing, two people in the same section at once, and cut it. The
    collaboration model that survived is asynchronous assignment, because
    live co-editing creates a question about who is attesting to a given
    answer, and on a KYB form that question has a legal answer.
  </Prose>
</Section>

<Section id="build" title="I built it as a working prototype instead of a Figma file.">
  <Prose>
    The flow has around thirty nodes, conditional branches, soft warnings,
    recapture loops, and states that depend on what someone answered four
    screens earlier. Figma prototyping links do not survive that. So I built it
    in HTML, CSS and vanilla JavaScript as a single hash-routed page, styled
    against Loop's real tokens, and put it under git.

    That decision paid for itself in a way I did not predict. Compliance,
    product, engineering and customer experience all reviewed the same
    artifact, and they could click the path their own question was about
    instead of asking me to demo it. Reviewers found branch problems I had not
    found, because they went where I would not have thought to click. Iteration
    became a commit rather than a duplicated frame, so there is a real history
    of what changed and when: five substantive iterations so far, and V3.2 is
    live at the moment.
  </Prose>

  <Prose>
    What I cut: agreements and signing moved out of the application entirely to
    after approval, because asking someone to sign a deposit account agreement
    for an account that might be declined is asking for a commitment the
    business has not made yet. Live registry verification during the form and
    an agent that screens uploads and requests the right documents are both
    designed and both scoped out of this release.
  </Prose>
</Section>

<Section id="standing" title="Where it stands.">
  <Prose>
    This is not shipped. The flow spec, the wireframes for every node including
    the exceptions, and the clickable prototype are the handoff package, and
    the compliance decisions that gate implementation are still open: which
    eligibility answers auto-deflect versus route to human review is owned by
    the compliance team, and the flow models both outcomes until they rule.

    There is also a real backlog. A long consolidated feedback list from the
    cross-functional reviews is still open, and closing it out before it grows
    is the current job.
  </Prose>
</Section>

<Section id="reflection" title="Reflection">
  <Prose>
    **The number that mattered was the one after approval.** I came in expecting
    the story to be about abandonment during the form, and there is a story
    there. But approved customers failing to activate is a worse problem,
    because those are people the business already said yes to, and no amount of
    form design fixes momentum lost during a thirty-four day wait.

    **Compliance is a design constraint, not an obstacle to route around.** Most
    of the fields are not negotiable. The order, the wording, the moment you
    ask, and what you do when the answer is no are all completely open, and
    that is where every improvement in this project came from.

    **A prototype people can click is a different object than a prototype
    people watch.** Building in code instead of Figma turned review meetings
    from me demoing into four teams exploring, and the branch problems they
    found were ones I could not have found alone.
  </Prose>
</Section>

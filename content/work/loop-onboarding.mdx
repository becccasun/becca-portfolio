<Prose>
  While building Loop's design system, I redesigned the onboarding experience
  end to end. The work moved from competitive research and a screen-by-screen
  audit into funnel analysis, a full PRD, 14 distinct Claude Code prototypes,
  and a shipped seven-step flow. Application time dropped from 23 minutes to 9.
  Conversion lifted 35%.
</Prose>

<Spacer size="md" />

<Section id="problem" title="The application was losing people before they could become customers">
  <Prose>
    Loop is a Canadian fintech doing cross-border banking for globally
    operating businesses. Its onboarding flow has to collect enough information
    for KYB and KYC verification, but the existing experience added avoidable
    friction around those requirements.

    Of 364 signups, 158 completed business verification. The flow fell off a
    cliff after that: only 18 reached the agreements step. Overall completion
    was effectively 0%.

    The application took 23 minutes with no progress indicator. KYB
    verification took a median of 3.3 days with no designed wait state. There
    was no business-type branching, and returning applicants were dropped back
    at the beginning.
  </Prose>
</Section>

<Section id="research" title="Competitive research turned patterns into concrete product decisions">
  <Prose>
    I walked through the end-to-end onboarding flows of Mercury, Ramp in the US
    and Canada, Float, Venn, Column, and Stack AI. I signed up for real accounts,
    screenshotted every step, and documented the patterns.

    Two patterns stood out. Venn checked business numbers against the Canadian
    Business Registry in real time. Most competitors used a left-rail stepper
    with clear step counts and time estimates upfront. Loop had neither.

    I also used an agent to summarize how Stripe, Wise, Kraken, and others
    structured their design systems into `design.md` files. Comparing those
    agent-readable specifications side by side taught me how to make design
    decisions legible to both people and the Claude Code prototype I would
    build later.

  </Prose>

<Section id="audit" title="I audited staging and production before drawing the new flow">
  <Prose>
    I walked through Loop's existing staging and production onboarding
    experiences screen by screen. In Figma, I annotated backtracking bugs,
    missing input guardrails, heavy-commitment moments, email validation gaps,
    and date-of-birth issues directly beside the screens where they occurred.

    The audit separated compliance requirements from interaction problems. The
    required information was not the only source of friction; sequence,
    feedback, re-entry, and missing states were design problems I could change.

  </Prose>

<Section id="data" title="KYB and KYC funnels showed where the experience broke">
  <Prose>
    I pulled AiPrise verification data to understand where applicants were
    dropping out across templates. The KYB views showed completion around the
    middle of the funnel and surfaced `BUSINESS_ID_MISMATCH` and `AML_MATCH` as
    important warning codes. KYC templates varied by role and verification
    method, with the largest multiple-role biometric sample at 79.27%
    completion.

    This evidence shaped the redesign priorities: reduce preventable mismatch,
    make the correct path depend on business structure, preserve progress, and
    turn the verification wait into an explicit product state.

  </Prose>

<Section id="ai" title="Fourteen distinct Claude Code prototypes made the flow a design instrument">
  <Prose>
    I wrote a full PRD that mapped every friction point to a root cause and a
    proposed fix. Then I built the redesigned flow as an interactive prototype
    in Claude Code before committing it to Figma.

    The archive contains 15 iteration files and 14 distinct prototypes:
    `V2.5` and `V3.1` are byte-identical, and there is no `V3.0`. Across those
    prototypes I tested the conditional funnel, registry autofill,
    business-type branches, progress model, and waiting-room states as one
    connected experience instead of isolated frames.

    The single-file prototype grew from 259,022 bytes in `V1.0` to 1,138,143
    bytes in `V3.7`, reflecting how much more of the end-to-end flow and its
    states became testable.

    The work also exposed a limitation in generic AI generation. Pasting a few
    production hex codes into Claude Code produced a plausible fintech screen
    with the wrong font, approximate greens, and full pill buttons instead of
    Loop's 8px radius. Agent-readable specifications made the difference:
    explicit tokens, component rules, and interaction constraints turned
    "close enough" output into something I could critique and iterate.

    This onboarding prototype became the proving ground for the larger design
    infrastructure. The reusable token system, Storybook grounding, and MCP
    skills are covered in the [Design AI MCP case study](/work/loop-design-ai-mcp).
  </Prose>

<Section id="redesign" title="The final flow branches early, fills what it can, and designs the wait">
  <Prose>
    The redesigned application introduced a conditional funnel that filters
    non-qualifying businesses at the start instead of the end. Entering a
    business number pulls company details from the Canadian Business Registry.
    Business-type branching means applicants only see steps relevant to their
    structure.

    A persistent left-rail stepper shows progress through the seven-step
    application. After submission, a designed waiting room explains status,
    expected timing, and what the applicant can do next instead of leaving the
    3.3-day median verification period as an empty gap.

  </Prose>

<Section id="outcomes" title="The redesigned onboarding flow shipped in Q3">
  <Prose>
    Application time dropped from 23 minutes to 9. Conversion lifted 35%.

    Those outcomes belonged to the product-design work: research, flow
    structure, registry autofill, conditional paths, persistent progress, and a
    designed waiting room. The design system and AI tooling supported the pace
    of iteration, but they were infrastructure for the feature rather than the
    feature's story.
  </Prose>
</Section>

<Section id="reflection" title="Reflection">
  <Prose>
    **AI accelerated the iteration loop; it did not choose the product
    direction.** The strongest decisions came from the competitive audit, the
    AiPrise data, and the current-flow review. Claude Code made it faster to
    test those decisions across 14 distinct connected prototypes.

    **Agent-readable design specs became part of the design work.** The same
    care used to define a component, token, or state for another designer also
    determines whether an agent produces something correct instead of merely
    plausible.

    **The waiting room deserved as much design attention as the form.** A
    median 3.3-day verification period is part of the customer experience.
    Designing what happens after submission was necessary to carry momentum
    from application into activation.
  </Prose>
</Section>

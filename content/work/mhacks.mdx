<Section id="overview" title="A hackathon that runs once a year, introduced by a page most applicants only ever see once.">
  <Prose>
    MHacks is the University of Michigan's flagship hackathon: 24 hours of
    creative engineering, design, building, and prototyping, drawing 1,000+
    student builders to Ann Arbor. The 2026 edition runs October 3–4.

    For most applicants the marketing site *is* MHacks until the doors open.
    It has to explain what the event is, when to apply, and why it's worth a
    weekend, and it has to do that in the few minutes between a link and a
    decision. I led design for the 2026 cycle as Design Chair.
  </Prose>

<Section id="concept" title="Build something that grows.">
  <Prose>
    The whole site hangs off one line — *build something that grows* — and one
    metaphor: a digital garden. A hackathon project starts as something small
    and half-finished, and the good ones keep going after the weekend ends.
    The garden gives that idea a form you can actually draw.

    The plants aren't generic. Every flower on the page is a Michigan
    species, labelled the way a botanical garden labels a specimen: common
    name, Latin binomial, and a status line. Apple Blossom *(Malus
    domestica,* introduced, state flower). Lily of the Valley *(Convallaria
    majalis,* invasive groundcover). Black-Eyed Susan *(Rudbeckia hirta,*
    native wildflower). Dwarf Lake Iris *(Iris lacustris,* native, state
    wildflower). Michigan Lily *(Lilium michiganense,* native wildflower).

    It's a small piece of writing that does a lot of work: it roots a
    national event in the specific place it happens, and it rewards the
    person who slows down enough to read a caption.
  </Prose>

<Section id="identity" title="A display serif over photography, and monospace for anything factual.">
  <Prose>
    The type system splits along a clear line. Anything expressive — the
    wordmark, section headings — is set in a high-contrast display serif.
    Anything factual — the countdown, deadline times, the plant labels, the
    MCP server URL — is monospace, letterspaced, and small. The reader can
    tell at a glance which register they're in.

    Colour stays in a narrow band: a deep forest green, a warm off-white, and
    the illustration palette on top. Sections alternate between the two
    grounds and are separated by large rounded corners, so scrolling the page
    feels like moving between beds in a garden rather than down a document.
  </Prose>

<Section id="sections" title="Timeline, sponsors, FAQ.">
  <Prose>
    The timeline is the most utilitarian thing on the page and gets the most
    typographic weight: five dates from applications opening on June 22
    through regular decisions on September 19, each one a large date on the
    left and a small monospace label on the right. Deadline times are spelled
    out to the minute and timezone, because that is the single most-asked
    question of any application cycle.

    The FAQ answers the next six: who can apply, cost, teams, what to build,
    when applications close, where it happens. Both sections are built to be
    scanned, not read.
  </Prose>

<Section id="agent" title="An application you can hand to your agent.">
  <Prose>
    MHacks 2026 ships an MCP server at `mhacks.org/mcp`. An applicant can
    connect Claude, Codex, or any MCP-capable client, and the agent reads the
    application schema, drafts answers with them, and submits — tied to their
    real MHacks login, with the MLH terms reviewed before anything is sent.

    Designing that meant designing for a reader who may never see the form.
    The section shows a real terminal transcript rather than a screenshot of
    a UI: the prompt, two tool calls resolving, and the agent stopping to ask
    for the school and t-shirt size it doesn't have. It sets the expectation
    that this is a conversation with checkpoints, not a black box that
    applies on your behalf.
  </Prose>

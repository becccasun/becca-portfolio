<Prose>
  I built Loop's first design system from scratch and wrapped it in an MCP
  layer that let 55 non-designers across engineering, growth, sales, and
  recruiting produce on-brand work without waiting on me. The onboarding
  redesign I built on top of the system cut application time from 23 minutes
  to 9 and lifted conversion by 35%.
</Prose>

<Spacer size="md" />

<Section id="problem" title="Loop was moving billions through a platform with no designer and no design system">
  <Prose>
    Loop is a Canadian fintech doing cross-border banking for globally
    operating businesses. Thousands of customers, billions flowing through the
    platform, regulated by FINTRAC. When I joined as the sole designer, the
    company had no design system, no component library, and no previous
    designer.

    The existing "system" was a Figma frame with hex codes on a canvas. Lilac,
    light blue, and light green buttons. No variables, no aliases, no component
    library. Engineers were pulling from Tailwind defaults because there was
    nothing else to pull from. Every screen in production had drifted.

    **Imagery pending**

    - Old Loop "design system": hex codes on a canvas with no component
      library, no variables, no token structure
    - Previous button components: lilac, light blue, and light green with no
      hierarchy or consistency
    - Previous component set: unstructured, no variants, no design tokens

    The surface problem was visual inconsistency. The real problem was
    throughput. As the only designer, every branded slide deck, every outreach
    email, every prototype scaffold, and every one-pager was a synchronous ask
    on me. My queue was becoming the company's queue.
  </Prose>
</Section>

<Section id="research" title="The first week: coffee chats, Slack channels, and finding where the friction actually lived">
  <Prose>
    In my first week I met with people across every team. Not formal
    interviews. Coffee chats, desk conversations, reading the Customer
    Feedback Slack channel obsessively. I wanted to understand what work people
    were doing that touched design and where it broke down.

    The pattern was blunt. Non-designers were not opening Figma. They were
    opening Slack and asking me. Growth was rebuilding pitch decks from
    screenshots. Recruiting was hand-styling job posts. Engineers were
    reaching for Tailwind defaults because the Figma library was not in their
    loop.

    That killed my first instinct, which was to build a Figma component library
    and evangelize adoption. A library nobody opens is a fiction.
  </Prose>
</Section>

<Section id="direction" title="Three options considered: the distribution layer won">
  <Prose>
    **A Figma-only library.** Publish tokens and components, train people on
    Figma. Killed it. The people who needed design output were not Figma users
    and were not going to become Figma users in the 12 weeks I had left.

    **A Storybook-first spec.** Let engineering own the system, designers pull
    from code. Rejected for v1. Non-designers still cannot operate Storybook,
    and I would have lost the Figma authoring surface I needed daily.

    **An agent-first system with an MCP distribution layer.** Figma as the
    authoring truth, Storybook as the engineering truth, and MCP skills as the
    layer that serves everyone else in the tool they already live in. Same
    system, three audiences, each in the tool they already use.

    I chose the third option. The bet: the distribution layer matters more than
    the library.
  </Prose>
</Section>

<Section id="references" title="Studying how other companies structure their design tokens and onboarding flows">
  <Prose>
    Before building, I studied how Stripe, Wise, Kraken, and others structured
    their design systems. I had an agent summarize their token architectures
    into design.md files so I could compare approaches side by side. That
    process taught me how to write agent-readable design specs, which became
    the foundation for the MCP skills I built later.

    For the onboarding redesign, I walked through the end-to-end flows of
    Mercury, Ramp (both US and Canada), Float, Venn, Column, and Stack AI. I
    signed up for real accounts, screenshotted every step, and documented
    patterns.

    **Imagery pending**

    - Competitive audit: Venn onboarding flow, signup through business
      verification
    - Competitive audit: Venn business details with real-time Canadian
      Business Registry lookup
    - Competitive audit: Float signup page and identity verification
    - Competitive audit: Column financial products selection and company
      employees steps
    - Competitive audit: Mercury post-submission waiting room with application
      timeline
    - Competitive audit: Ramp product selection, submit page, and account
      verification
    - Competitive audit: Stack AI login

    Two patterns stood out. Venn checked business numbers against the Canadian
    Business Registry in real time. And most competitors used a left-rail
    stepper with clear step counts and time estimates upfront. Loop had
    neither.
  </Prose>
</Section>

<Section id="system" title="Building the token architecture: brand, alias, mapping">
  <Prose>
    I built a tertiary token structure: brand tokens at the base, alias tokens
    in the middle, mapping tokens at the surface. This sounds like overkill for
    a Series A, and the tradeoff was real. Heavier setup, longer runway to the
    first shipped component.

    I defended it because the alias layer is what makes a single token dress a
    pitch deck, an outreach email, a one-pager, and a mobile prototype from one
    source. Without it, the MCP layer would have been impossible.
  </Prose>

  <Figure

    wide
  />

  <Prose>
    **Imagery pending**

    - Loop color token matrix in light and dark themes, showing brand, alias,
      and mapping layers
    - Figma variables panels with brand variables organized by primary, gray,
      error, warning, success, and information
    - Typography system: DM Sans type ramp driven entirely by variables, not
      styles
    - Color foundations page with accessibility contrast ratios

    The type system uses DM Sans, bound to brand-tier variables for font-size,
    line-height, and tracking. Nothing is hand-typed. Change a variable and
    every instance updates.

    I built 200+ tokens and components, published them as Loop's single source
    of truth in Figma, and synced them to Storybook through a token pipeline.
    That pipeline is the boring plumbing that makes everything else work. If
    the design system drifts from code, engineers stop trusting it, and the
    whole MCP layer collapses.
  </Prose>
</Section>

<Section id="skills" title="Fifteen narrow skills, not one big agent">
  <Prose>
    Each MCP skill has one job. Compose a branded pitch deck. Generate a
    dynamic email template. Spin up a one-pager. Scaffold a mobile prototype.
    Run an ICP friction review.

    **For engineers:** On-brand mobile prototype scaffolds and UI generation
    synced with the codebase. The MCP does not just hand over tokens. It syncs
    with Loop's codebase, so a prototype spun up in Claude resembles code that
    could actually ship.

    **For the growth team:** Dynamic email templates, one-pagers, and pitch
    decks so they can focus on personalized outreach instead of formatting.

    **For recruiting and operations:** Branded job posts, ICP friction reviews,
    and internal comms assets.

    I wrote an internal rule early: AI-generated designs are only allowed when
    we need to sketch a generic prototype fast or when front-end features need
    to ship for testing and a joint engineering-design effort is not timely.
    Anything backend, anything touching real user data, stays human and gets
    audited by the risks team. Without that rule, the MCP would have become the
    fastest way to ship subtly wrong things at Loop.
  </Prose>
</Section>

<Section id="onboarding" title="Redesigning onboarding: 0% completion to a shipped flow that cut application time by 60%">
  <Prose>
    While building the design system, I also redesigned Loop's onboarding. The
    existing funnel was losing people at every step. Of 364 signups, 158
    completed business verification. The flow fell off a cliff after that:
    only 18 reached the agreements step. Overall completion was effectively 0%.

    **Auditing the existing flow and documenting every friction point**

    I walked through Loop's existing staging and production onboarding flows
    screen by screen, annotating issues on sticky notes directly in Figma.

    **Imagery pending**

    - Existing Loop staging environment: full audit with sticky-note
      annotations on every screen
    - Existing Loop production environment: full screen inventory
    - Figma audit workspace with annotations on backtracking bugs, input
      guardrails, and UX friction
    - Audit annotations flagging heavy commitment, email validation, and
      date-of-birth guardrail issues

    **The KYB/KYC funnel data that shaped the redesign priorities**

    I pulled analytics from the AiPrise verification dashboard to understand
    exactly where applicants were dropping off across different verification
    templates.

    **Imagery pending**

    - KYB journey funnels showing completion rates across June and July
    - KYB warning code data with BUSINESS_ID_MISMATCH and AML_MATCH
    - KYC funnels for beneficial owner, director, document, and multiple-role
      verification
    - KYC warning code, country, and ID-type breakdowns

    **The redesigned flow: conditional funnels, registry autofill, and a
    designed waiting room**

    I wrote a full PRD mapping every friction point to a root cause and a
    proposed fix. The biggest problems: the application took 23 minutes with
    no progress indicator, KYB verification took a median of 3.3 days with no
    designed wait state, there was no business-type branching, and re-entry
    after leaving mid-flow dropped people back to the beginning.

    I built an interactive prototype in Claude Code (17 iterations, each an
    improvement on the last) to test the redesigned flow before committing to
    Figma. The final design introduced:

    A conditional funnel that filters non-qualifying businesses at the start
    instead of at the end. Autofill from the Canadian Business Registry, so
    entering a business number pulls in company details automatically.
    Business-type branching so each applicant only sees the steps relevant to
    their structure. A designed waiting room with clear status, expected
    timeline, and things the applicant can do while they wait. A persistent
    left-rail stepper showing progress.

    **Imagery pending**

    - Redesigned seven-step application flow
    - Redesigned waiting room flow
    - Figma onboarding iterations and three flow variants
    - KYC/KYB verification screens with selfie and ID capture
    - Interactive prototype: final shipped version V3.7

    The redesign shipped to production in Q3. Application time dropped from 23
    minutes to 9. Conversion lifted 35%.
  </Prose>
</Section>

<Section id="reflection" title="What I would do differently">
  <Prose>
    **Start with three skills, not fifteen.** I built the full slate before I
    had adoption data on which requests were actually recurring. A couple of
    the fifteen are still theoretical. If I did this again, I would ship three
    skills, measure which ones people actually use, and expand from there.

    **Measure usage depth, not just access.** 55 people have access to the MCP
    tools. I did not measure how frequently each person used them before my
    internship ended. Access is not the same as adoption, and I left without
    the data to tell the difference.

    **Automated skill drift detection.** As the brand or product evolves, the
    15 skills have to stay in sync with the token pipeline and codebase. There
    is no automated test that catches a stale skill yet. That is the biggest
    maintenance risk I left behind.
  </Prose>
</Section>

<Section id="principles" title="Two things I am carrying into every design role after this">
  <Prose>
    **Solo designer means build force multipliers, not more screens.** My
    leverage was not in shipping more UI. It was in building infrastructure
    that lets a company operate design without me. Every hour I spent on the
    MCP saved a dozen future hours of ad-hoc asset requests from my Slack.

    **AI gets you ten options. Judgment is still yours.** Every skill on this
    MCP is a machine for generating on-brand work fast. What made them
    ship-worthy was not the generation. It was the token architecture, the
    layout templates I seeded, and the rule that kept AI out of backend and
    off customer-facing surfaces. Distribution without taste is just more
    noise, faster.
  </Prose>
</Section>

<Section id="overview" title="What do you do when your product's whole advantage is invisible for the first ten minutes?">
  <Prose>
    Embedder is a Y Combinator-backed AI assistant built for firmware and
    embedded systems work. General coding tools are software-first: they have
    no datasheet awareness, no register-level understanding, no idea what
    board you are talking to. Embedder does. It reads hardware context and
    runs in the terminal alongside the device, which is the entire reason it
    exists.

    None of that was visible when you installed it.

    Embedder's founder brought this to Atlas Digital Technology Consulting at
    the University of Michigan as a research question rather than a design
    brief. Not "make this prettier" but "why do people bounce off it, and what
    is realistic to promise them?" Six of us worked on it from February to
    April 2026. I was one of two project managers, and I owned the client
    relationship and scope, designed the research, ran sessions and coded
    transcripts, and worked on the synthesis and the final report.
  </Prose>
</Section>

<Section id="outcome" title="What changed.">
  <Prose>
    Embedder used the findings to shape their next round of onboarding
    updates. The research also fed two decisions beyond the immediate
    interface: whether a native app was worth building, and how to structure
    user research going forward so they could keep doing this without us.

    That last one is the outcome I did not expect and value most. We were
    hired to answer a question about onboarding. What the client also took
    from it was a way of asking questions.
  </Prose>
</Section>

<Section id="method" title="We narrowed the brief before we ran a single session.">
  <Prose>
    The founder's opening ask was product-market fit across 50 to 100
    participants, weighted toward professional automotive engineers in
    Detroit. Data saturation in qualitative work tends to arrive around
    twenty, and we had ten weeks and six people, most of us new to firmware.
    Chasing the larger number would have bought us a thinner version of the
    same answer.

    So we cut it. The engagement became onboarding and feature discovery,
    scoped to the first ten minutes, and the participant target came down to
    a number we could actually reach and code properly. I took that back to
    the client rather than quietly under-delivering on the original.

    What we ran:

    - **Twenty usability sessions**, remote over Zoom, with CE, EE and
      robotics students and firmware engineers. Participants installed
      Embedder themselves and worked through tasks built on real embedded
      debugging scenarios: I2C, Timer and PWM, EXTI interrupts.
    - **A comparative analysis** against Cursor, Codex and Claude Code,
      scored on twelve criteria drawn from the interviews rather than from a
      feature list.
    - **A heuristic evaluation** of both the terminal and the VS Code
      extension, run in parallel against Nielsen's heuristics.

    Before any of it, I spent a session with an embedded engineer learning
    what firmware development actually involves end to end. The client had
    been explicit that general CS majors were not suitable participants
    because they would not know the specifics. The same applied to us. A
    researcher who cannot tell a serial monitor from a chat panel cannot hear
    what a participant is telling them.
  </Prose>
</Section>

<Section id="finding" title="Every participant reached for the same word: wrapper.">
  <Prose>
    Users described Embedder as a "Claude wrapper", as "just a chat box", as
    something you "could replicate on your own quite easily". One put it
    flatly: they saw no advantage in using Embedder over Claude Code in any
    of the trials they had just run.

    They were wrong. But they were wrong for a reason the product handed
    them. Over 60% never found slash commands, mode switching or the
    attachments menu on their own. Half flagged the Monitor or console as
    confusing, unable to tell the device-facing surface from the AI chat. One
    participant said the Monitor confused them because it sat separate from
    everything else and they still could not work out what it did. Another
    only made sense of it by mapping it onto something he already knew,
    assuming it must be a serial monitor.

    None of this was surprising on its own. Onboarding is rough in most early
    products, and we expected to find friction. What made it worth the ten
    weeks was the second-order effect.
  </Prose>

  <PullQuote>
    When the differentiator is invisible, users do not conclude the product is
    confusing. They conclude it is redundant, and price it against tools they
    already have for free.
  </PullQuote>

  <Prose>
    That reframed the whole engagement. We came in treating this as a
    usability problem and left describing it as a positioning problem
    expressed through the interface. The features mostly worked once people
    found them. Nothing in the product argued for itself.

    The heuristic evaluation sharpened it further. Embedder is built for
    developers who live in a terminal, and yet filtering, clearing, switching
    panels and approving permissions all required a mouse. A tool that claims
    to be built for developers and then breaks their most basic expectation
    is not just adding friction. It is telling them it does not understand
    them.
  </Prose>
</Section>

<Section id="recommendations" title="Seven recommendations, ordered by what they would cost to ignore.">
  <Prose>
    We delivered seven, each tied to the sessions and heuristics that
    produced it rather than to taste. The load-bearing ones:

    - **A guided first-run flow, plus a walkthrough video on the marketplace
      listing.** Users arrived with no mental model of the console, the chat
      or the hardware requirement, and competitors already set the
      expectation of being handed one.
    - **Separate and relabel the two surfaces.** "Send to device" against
      "Ask Embedder", so nobody has to guess which panel talks to hardware.
    - **A keyboard path to everything.** A command palette, shortcuts and
      autocomplete for slash commands, so the differentiated features are
      reachable by the users most likely to appreciate them.
    - **Align to the conventions users already carry.** Their mental models
      come from Claude Code, Copilot, Cursor and the Arduino IDE. Embedder
      does not need to invent interaction patterns, and every place it did
      cost a feature discovery.
    - **Start people in Plan mode.** Act mode executed file changes by
      default and most participants did not know it was on. A review layer
      nobody knows about is not a review layer.

    The through-line is that almost none of this asks for new features. It
    asks for the existing ones to be legible.
  </Prose>
</Section>

<Section id="limits" title="We tested a hardware tool without hardware.">
  <Prose>
    This is the honest limit of the study and it belongs in the middle of the
    page rather than buried in an appendix. Sessions ran remotely with no
    board connected. Participants installed Embedder, explored the interface
    and worked through debugging scenarios as written tasks, but never
    watched the tool actually read a device.

    Which means we studied the first ten minutes thoroughly and never studied
    the moment the product proves itself. Our central finding is that users
    could not see what made Embedder different. We had built a study in which
    the most convincing evidence was unavailable to them. Some part of the
    "wrapper" reaction is a real product problem and some part is an artifact
    of how we tested, and this study cannot separate the two.

    I flagged it in the report rather than letting the findings read as
    stronger than they were.
  </Prose>
</Section>

<Section id="reflection" title="Reflection">
  <Prose>
    **Get hardware in the room, or say plainly what the absence costs.** If I
    ran this again I would fight harder for even two or three sessions with a
    board connected, as a check on the other twenty. The constraint was real,
    and I still under-weighted how much it shaped the headline finding.

    **Learn the domain before writing the guide, not during.** The client
    ruled out participants who did not know firmware. Applying that same bar
    to the research team changed the questions we asked. You cannot follow up
    on an interesting answer you did not recognise as interesting.

    **A scope cut you take to the client is a different object than one you
    make quietly.** Narrowing from a hundred participants to twenty could
    have read as falling short. Framed as a choice, with saturation as the
    reasoning, it became the thing that let the work go deep enough to be
    worth acting on.

    **The most durable deliverable was not the report.** They used the
    findings for onboarding, which is what we were hired for. They also took
    a structure for running research themselves, which nobody asked for and
    which outlasts every specific recommendation in the document.
  </Prose>
</Section>

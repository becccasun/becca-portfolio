<Section id="overview" title="A division that runs 24/7 across three campuses, recruiting through a page that explained none of it.">
  <Prose>
    The University of Michigan's Division of Public Safety and Security runs
    police, security and emergency management across Ann Arbor, Dearborn and
    Flint. They came to Atlas Digital, a student consultancy at Michigan, to
    rebuild the hiring experience they call "A Great Place to Work."

    Five of us worked on it: a project manager, a lead designer, and three
    designers. I was one of the designers. I owned the hiring landing page and
    the design system, and shared the job listing and saved-applications
    screens with another designer.
  </Prose>
</Section>

<Section id="problem" title="Applying meant logging in to find out you could not.">
  <Prose>
    We audited the existing site and worked through the DPSS team's own view of
    who they were trying to reach. Three problems kept surfacing.

    The copy repeated itself across pages without answering the question an
    applicant actually has. Navigation gave no reliable sense of where you
    were. And the application flow put a university login in front of external
    candidates, which is a wall standing exactly where DPSS said it least
    wanted one.

    None of it was a visual problem. It was a sequencing problem: what a
    candidate needs in order to decide sat behind a process built for people
    who had already decided.
  </Prose>
</Section>

<Section id="work" title="I put the hiring process, with its real durations, on the landing page.">
  <Prose>
    The strongest move was the least decorative. DPSS hires in four phases:
    first interview, panel interview, background check, administrative panel.
    Each carries a genuine duration, and background investigation for a public
    safety role runs to weeks rather than days.

    That timeline used to be invisible until you were already inside it.
    Putting it on the landing page as four labelled phases with their durations
    stated answers the question every candidate has and most career pages
    avoid printing. It also reframes a slow process as a thorough one, which is
    the honest reading for a division that runs armed patrol.
  </Prose>

  <Prose>
    On the listing side the constraint was volume. DPSS posts a wide spread of
    roles, from operations analysts to control systems technicians to safety
    outreach. Filters for location, job type and date posted do the narrowing,
    and each posting can be saved or shared from its own row.

    Saving is the part that answers the login wall. A candidate can gather what
    interests them and track what they have submitted through a "My
    Applications" tab, without an account being the price of entry.
  </Prose>

<Section id="system" title="Four people drawing screens in parallel needed one file to agree on.">
  <Prose>
    The risk in a team this size is four plausible interfaces that do not look
    related. I built the design system that held them together: the palette
    anchored on Michigan maize and navy, three button tiers, a type scale, and
    the components that recur across every screen.

    It is not a large system and it did not need to be. It needed to exist
    early enough that nobody had to invent a button.
  </Prose>

<Section id="outcome" title="Where it landed.">
  <Prose>
    We delivered personas, wireframes, three high-fidelity prototypes and the
    design system, presented to DPSS at a final review. The engagement was
    scoped to design rather than build, so what DPSS took away was an argument
    in prototype form for what their hiring experience could be.

    Feasibility shaped it throughout. We asked in every client meeting what
    their platform and their team could actually carry, because a
    recommendation that cannot be built on the system they already run is not a
    recommendation.
  </Prose>
</Section>

<Section id="reflection" title="Reflection">
  <Prose>
    **The most useful thing I designed was a table of durations.** Not the
    hero, not the palette. Telling candidates that a background check takes
    about two weeks is the kind of detail organisations omit by reflex, and
    stating it publicly does more for trust than any amount of photography.

    **A design system's value is in when it arrives, not how complete it is.**
    Mine was small. It landed before the other screens were drawn, which is the
    only reason four designers' work reads as one product.

    **Public sector constraints are the brief, not an obstacle.** Accessibility
    requirements and the limits of the existing platform were fixed before we
    started. Treating them as the shape of the problem rather than a tax on it
    is what made the handoff something DPSS could act on.
  </Prose>
</Section>
